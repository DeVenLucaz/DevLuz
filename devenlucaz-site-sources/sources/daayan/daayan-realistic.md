DAAYAN: बंधन
A Novel by DeVenLucaz

======================================================================


--------------------------------------------------
About This Book
--------------------------------------------------
He did not plan any of it.

Not the first night. Not the years that followed. Not the part where he became someone he did not recognise, and not the part where he walked away from everything he had built — with three thousand rupees in his pocket and sandals on his feet, into a city he had never been to.

He did not plan to meet her on the back of a bike at 4 AM. He did not plan to need her. He certainly did not plan to spend years mistaking the shape of what he felt for something it was not.

DAAYAN: बंधन is a story set in Pune — in its office corridors and late-night tea stalls, in the sealed world of the night shift, in the specific silence of a city at 4 AM when the roads belong to nobody. It follows Pruthvi through five chapters of one relationship that was never simple enough to be named and never distant enough to be forgotten.

Daayan is five years older. She is not the villain of this story.

She never was.

This is not a love story. It is something harder to explain and longer to leave.

Some bonds do not break. They just change what they are holding.


--------------------------------------------------
The People of This Story
--------------------------------------------------

[ PRUTHVI ]

Mid-twenties. Night shift. The kind of person who is always slightly more present than the situation requires — more attentive, more intense, more certain. He is not cruel. He is someone who loves without knowing how to hold what he loves without squeezing.

He has two versions of himself — the one his office gang sees, and the one that only exists around her. He has spent years making sure these two versions never meet.

He narrates parts of this book himself. When he does, he is honest. But honesty and accuracy are not always the same thing.


[ DAAYAN ]

Five years older. Dark saree, always. A specific way of going quiet when something is wrong that people who do not know her miss entirely. She does not explain herself often. She has learned that explanations become ammunition.

She is not supernatural. She is simply someone who exists at a frequency that certain people cannot stop tuning into — even years later, even from other cities, even when they are trying very hard not to.


--------------------------------------------------
A Note
--------------------------------------------------
This story is true.

The city is real. The office building is real. The night shift is real. The corridor where two people stood at a strange angle to each other and took the best photograph of their lives — that is real.

The names are not.

Everything you feel while reading this — that is also real. If you recognise the shape of it from your own life, that is not a coincidence. Some things are universal precisely because they are never spoken out loud.

Read it the way you read things that are true.

— DeVenLucaz

CHAPTER ONE
पहली बार
(The First Time)

======================================================================


--- 1.1 — The Igloo and the Night ---

There is a version of Pune that most people never see.

Not because it is hidden — it is right there, on the same roads, in the same buildings. But it only exists between 1 AM and 5 AM, and most people are asleep for it.

The office floor at 3 AM is its own universe. Outside, the city has gone quiet — the last autos have found their final fares, the tea stalls have pulled their shutters, even the stray dogs have settled into their corners. But inside this floor, everything is awake. The hum of servers. The cold of the AC set too high because nobody senior is around to complain. The specific intimacy of people who share a version of the world that the daytime does not know about.

Pruthvi has worked nights long enough that the daytime feels like a rumour.

He knows this floor the way you know a place you have lived in long enough that your body navigates it without instruction. He knows which chair has the wobbly leg. He knows which corner of the break room gets signal. He knows the sound the lift makes at 4 AM when it is the only thing moving in the building.

He knows the people here the way you know people when the performance of the day has gone to sleep along with everyone else. Night shift does this — strips away the version people maintain for office hours and leaves something more honest underneath.

He knows her as hi-bye. A face on the same floor. A name attached to a desk he passes on the way to his own.

This is about to change.

It is a Fun Friday — the thing offices do when they want their employees to feel like the walls are optional. A turf somewhere, organised by HR, attendance technically voluntary and therefore mandatory in the way that voluntary things always are when your manager is watching.

Pruthvi goes. He is sociable enough to enjoy these things and self-aware enough to know that being seen at them matters.

He does not own a bike. This has never been a problem before tonight.

Someone in the group — it will later be impossible to remember exactly who — says: she lives on your route. Take him.

She is Daayan. She has a bike. She lives on his route.

These are the only facts that matter at 4 AM when the event winds down and Pune is doing its slow exhale into almost-morning.

They leave together.


--- 1.2 — The Hair Clutcher ---

The first half of the ride is silent.

Not awkward-silent. Just the silence of two people who are hi-bye colleagues and have not yet found the reason to be more. The city moves past them — empty roads, orange streetlights, the occasional truck making its way somewhere important. Her bike cuts through the 4 AM air with the ease of someone who has ridden these roads enough times to stop thinking about them.

Then it starts.

Her hair is open under her jacket, and the wind has other ideas. It begins pulling strands loose — first a few, then more — until they are blowing directly into his face. Not gently. With the specific enthusiasm of hair that has been freed from containment and is making the most of it.

He endures it for a moment. Then another.

Then: "Yaar — your hair is literally attacking me."

She does not slow down. She does not look back. But he can see, from the slight shift of her shoulders, that she is trying not to laugh.

"Left pocket of my jacket," she says. "There's a clutcher."

He finds it. A small hair clutcher, the kind that lives in jacket pockets of people who have learned to always have one. He holds it for a moment — this small domestic object that he has been handed without ceremony — and then, carefully, reaches forward.

It is not a simple thing to tie someone's hair while they are riding a bike at 4 AM on an empty Pune road. It requires both hands. It requires proximity. It requires a specific careful attention that has nothing to do with the task and everything to do with who you are paying it to.

He manages it, eventually. Not perfectly. But enough.

"Done," he says.

"Thank you." A pause. Then: "You took forever."

"I was being careful."

"Of what?"

He does not have an answer for this. But something in the not-having-an-answer is where the conversation starts.

They talk. About nothing important — the office, the people at the event, some piece of gossip that one of them has been holding onto since Tuesday. He is talkative, she is talkative, and they discover this about each other in the way you discover things about people at 4 AM: all at once, without the usual filters.

They miss his stop.

Not dramatically. Not even noticeably, at first. One moment they are on the road that leads to his building, and then somehow they are not, because neither of them suggested slowing down and so the bike did not slow down and the conversation did not slow down and his building fell behind them in the dark.

She realises first. She circles back without making it a thing.

Outside his society gate, she stops.

They sit there for five minutes — barely five minutes — talking about nothing and everything, the way conversations go when they have found their rhythm and the rhythm does not want to stop.

Then: "Okay. Good night."

"Good night."

She leaves. He goes upstairs.

He does not think about her again that night.

This is the last time that will be true.

--- 1.3 — Okay Fine, I'll Come ---

She texted first.

Saturday afternoon. I was doing what I did on weekend afternoons — nothing, specifically and deliberately. The night shift had a way of inverting the body's relationship with time, and weekends were when I tried to remind myself that the sun existed.

Her message: I'm going out somewhere. If you want you can come.

I read it. I put the phone down. My body and my bed were in complete agreement about the correct response to this invitation.

I did not reply.

She called. I let it ring.

She called again. I picked up. "Yaar I'm tired, some other time —"

"It'll be fun. Just come."

"I'm literally horizontal right now."

"So get vertical. I'm coming to pick you up in twenty minutes."

She hung up.

I stared at my ceiling for thirty seconds, then got up.

This is how it started. Not with a decision. With a girl who did not take no for an answer and a boy who was too lazy to argue.

She took me to a mall on the other side of the city. One of the big ones — the kind that becomes its own ecosystem on weekends, with events running on the ground floor and people sitting in cafes on the upper levels watching the events from above.

We walked. We sat. We got coffee at one place, quick bites at another, and then just sat on a regular bench in the middle of the crowd like two people who had nowhere to be and were fine about it.

She lied to me about smoking.

I found this out at the smoking corner. I had stepped out for a cigarette and she came with me, lit up, and smoked with the specific confidence of someone who had been doing it for years. I said nothing. Later — much later — I found out she did not smoke at all. Had never smoked. Had simply decided, at that moment, that she wanted to stay in the conversation and the conversation was happening at the smoking corner.

I found this out and I laughed for a long time.

Then I thought about what it meant — that she had stood in the cold, inhaling something she did not want, just to stay beside me.

I filed it away. I had already started a drawer for things about her.

We were there from early evening until past eleven.

When we finally came out the city had shifted into its late-night version — quieter, more honest, the kind of Pune that only shows itself after the crowds have gone home. We sat outside for a while longer, near her society entrance, talking about things I cannot fully reconstruct now.

Memories, mostly. Her past, my past, the specific pleasure of telling your life to someone who is hearing it for the first time. She was a good talker. She was not a good listener — she had too many of her own thoughts to let someone else's sit undisturbed for long. But she was the right kind of not-good-listener, the kind who interrupts because they are interested rather than because they are waiting for their turn.

She dropped me home close to midnight.

I went upstairs. I fell asleep almost immediately.

Something had started. I did not know what to call it yet.

I would spend a long time not knowing what to call it.

  She lied about smoking to stay beside him.
  He found out years later and understood immediately.
  The drawer of things about her was already full.
  He just had not looked at what was in it.


--- 1.4 — Max Noticed First ---

Max had been watching.

This was his nature — he was the kind of person whose attention was a precision instrument, and he had pointed it at Pruthvi and Daayan and arrived at conclusions that Pruthvi had not yet arrived at himself.

On the Monday after the mall night, he appeared beside Pruthvi's desk with the expression of someone who has been waiting to say something and is now choosing his moment.

"So," Max said.

"So," Pruthvi said.

"How was the weekend?"

"Fine."

A pause. Max's version of a pause was not like other people's pauses. It had weight.

"You should go home with her after shift," he said. "On her bike."

"She goes that way anyway."

"I know. That's why I said it."

Pruthvi looked at him. Max looked back with the specific neutrality of someone who has made his point and is content to let it do its work.

The next day, at end of shift, Pruthvi found himself at the bike parking with no particular plan.

She was already there. She looked up. He made a gesture that meant nothing specific but communicated clearly: I don't have a way home.

She made a gesture back that meant: obviously, get on.

That was how it started — not with a conversation or a decision but with two gestures in a parking lot at the end of a night shift.

After that the routine assembled itself without being arranged. He came home on her bike. When he got late the next day and could not find transport, he called her and she came. This became the assumption — not discussed, not named, just: this is how it is now.

In the office everything was together. Every break, every smoke break, every slow hour of the night shift when the work had quieted down and the floor belonged to them and the handful of other people who lived in this sealed nocturnal world.

Not like a couple. This distinction mattered to both of them, though neither said it. Like besties — the specific closeness of two people who have chosen each other's company above all available alternatives and are doing so loudly enough that everyone on the floor has noticed.

The fire had caught. It was not yet what it would become.

That came later.


  — ✦ —
  Max noticed before either of them did.
  This is how it usually goes — the outside eye
  sees the shape of things before the people inside them
  have stopped moving long enough to look.


--- 1.5 — The Week at Rohan's ---

My friend Rohan's wife was going to her hometown for a week.

He called it a bro week. Made it sound like an event. I said yes without thinking much about it — it was a week, it was Rohan, it was the kind of casual plan that friends make without ceremony.

I told Daayan.

I told her as information. Not as a question, not as something I was seeking her opinion on. We were friends. Friends inform each other of things.

She went quiet.

Her specific quiet — the one where the temperature drops a few degrees and you feel it before you understand it.

"Don't go," she said.

"It's just a week."

"I know. Don't go."

I remember being confused by this. We were friends. I had friends. Friends had other friends. This was not complicated.

"I'll be back in a week," I said. "It's fine."

She did not argue further. She went quiet in a different way — the way of someone who has said what they wanted to say and is now waiting to see what you do with it.

I went to Rohan's.

The second day was the office function. Traditional dress day — one of those HR events that everyone complains about and then secretly enjoys. The building was full of colour and noise and the specific energy of people who have been given permission to be less professional for an afternoon.

I got there and found the floor.

She was in the corridor with her colleagues. Taking pictures. Laughing at something on someone's phone. The group was animated, the light in the corridor was good, everyone was doing the thing you do at office functions which is performing enjoyment whether or not you feel it.

I went toward her.

She looked up. She saw me. She looked back down.

I thought: okay, she's busy.

I tried again ten minutes later. Same thing.

And again. Same thing.

Now something shifted in me — not hurt exactly, not yet, more like confusion with an edge to it. We had spent every free minute together for weeks. We had a language. And she was using that language right now to say something I did not know how to read.


--- 1.6 — The Corridor and The Photograph ---

He found her in a corner corridor.

The building was a pentagon — one of those corporate structures that prioritised geometry over practicality, full of angled corridors that led to unexpected corners. The corner he found her in was one of those — open to the air on one side, private enough that the sound of the function did not quite reach.

He took her hand.

Not gently. Not forcefully. Just — took it, the way you take someone's hand when you have decided that the conversation you need to have requires their full attention.

"What is happening," he said. Not a question. A statement handed to her.

She looked at him. Her eyes were full in the way of someone who has been holding something carefully for several days and is now very close to the limit of what careful can do.

"It's been a long time we are always together," she said. "And you made this decision without asking me. I didn't like it."

Pruthvi stood very still.

Inside: a feeling he had no name for. Not guilt — he had felt guilt before and this was different. Not love — he had felt that too, or believed he had, and this was also different. It was more like the moment you realise you are standing in a room you did not know you had walked into. The door is behind you. The room is warm. You have been here long enough that leaving would require an effort you are not sure you want to make.

Outside: calm. Measured. The version of himself he had learned to deploy when something was happening that he did not fully understand — present, steady, not showing the inside.

"I'll come back tomorrow," he said. "First thing."

She nodded. Not fully satisfied. But she nodded.

He was back at Rohan's that evening to pack.

He was back in Pune the next morning.

He had packed in eight minutes. He did not examine the speed.

After everything was said, they moved to the main entrance corridor — the one facing the office door, the only stretch of space that felt like theirs right now, where the function noise receded to the background.

She took out her phone.

She held it out to him. A wordless instruction.

He took it. She stepped beside him. Her hand found his waist — easy, natural, without announcement. He put his arm around her shoulder, gently, the way you hold something you have just realised you do not want to drop.

He pressed the shutter.

The function was happening somewhere behind them. The corridor light was ordinary. Neither of them looked like they were trying to look like anything.

It was the best picture of theirs.

He still has it. He knows exactly where it is. His phone wallpaper is a different picture — a fun moment, both of them laughing at something — but the corridor photograph exists in its own folder, in its own category of things that are kept without needing to be looked at.

Some photographs are not for looking. They are for knowing they exist.


  — ✦ —
  He came back the next morning. Eight minutes to pack.
  She had asked him not to go.
  He had gone anyway, and come back anyway.
  Neither of them talked about what this meant.
  The photograph existed. That was enough, for now.

~ ~ ~ DAAYAN ~ ~ ~

  She knew, when she said don't go, that she had no right to say it.

  They were friends. She understood this completely and said it anyway.

  What she could not explain was that his absence felt like a test she had not agreed to take. They had built something in those weeks — something with a specific texture and warmth. And he had walked away from it as if it were furniture rather than architecture.

  When he came back the next morning she felt something she did not let herself name.

  She took out her phone and asked him to take a picture. This was the most honest thing she knew how to do with what she was feeling.

  She looked at the picture afterwards. She looked good in it. So did he.

  She saved it and said nothing.

CHAPTER TWO
हमारा वक्त
(Our Time)

======================================================================


--- 2.1 — Two Worlds, One Schedule ---

Night shift has its own logic.

The world outside runs on a schedule that night shift workers observe from a distance, like watching a film in a language you understand but do not speak. Meetings happen in the morning. Decisions get made in afternoons. The city's official life — traffic, commerce, the visible performance of productivity — runs its course while the night shift is asleep.

And then at 9 PM the igloo opens.

This is what Pruthvi called it, privately. The igloo. A sealed world with its own temperature, its own population, its own rules. The floor at 3 AM was genuinely different from the floor at 3 PM — quieter and louder at the same time, the quiet of fewer people and the loud of no one watching, the specific freedom of a workplace after the management has gone home.

He had been living in the igloo long enough that the daytime felt borrowed. His real hours were these.

She had been in the igloo too. Different department, same hours, same sealed world.

Now she was leaving it.

They absconded together.

One day they looked at each other and a four-minute conversation produced a decision that changed several things. They did not go in the next morning.

The phones started immediately. Alstern, Paige, Samuel, Max — the inner circle, the people Pruthvi had built relationships with carefully and maintained well, the people who knew him as reliable, dependable, the version of himself he presented during business hours.

They knew, of course, that the two of them had left together. An office is a closed system. Information travels at the speed of observation.

By that day they had convinced him to return. He went back.

She did not.

He filed this difference away. He was building a habit of filing things.

He returned to the office. She returned to her flat and to the specific freedom of having no schedule. Different departments, different choices, different versions of the same decision.

Pruthvi went back to the igloo. He maintained his office gang — Alstern who had become something like a mentor, Paige who he had started calling didi in the way you give someone a title that means something, Samuel the admin who knew everything about everyone, Max who noticed things.

He maintained these relationships carefully. He maintained Daayan carefully.

He had learned, without being taught, to keep his worlds from touching.


  — ✦ —
  He went back. She didn't.
  He told himself this was just different choices.
  He did not examine what it meant that he always returned
  and she always found a way to leave.


--- 2.2 — Fevi Quick ---

What followed was the fevi quick phase.

This was Daayan's word for it — fevi quick, the adhesive, the one that bonds permanently. She said it as a joke during one of their early morning conversations, after his shift ended and she had come to pick him up and they had ended up sitting on the stairs of her building for two hours instead of going inside.

She was jobless. Her schedule was entirely free. Her friend lived downstairs in the same building — a constant presence who would come up or be gone at various hours — and other than that her days were hers to arrange as she chose.

She chose them around him.

His night shift ended at the hour when Pune was just beginning to wake up. The milkmen had been. The first autos were on the road. The sky was the specific colour it turns when the night has finished but the day has not fully committed. This was when he called her. This was when she came.

Nobody else existed at this hour. Not really.

They had no single place they went. This was the thing about those months — they were known everywhere and tied to nowhere. Multiple tea stalls across the neighbourhood, and at each one the owner had learned their order without being asked. A table that was understood to be theirs when they arrived. A gesture of recognition that required no words.

The neighbourhood knew them the way neighbourhoods know people who appear together consistently — not as a category, just as a fact. Those two. Always around.

His flat. Her flat. The building stairs. The road between. The tea stalls. The random walls and steps and corners of a city that becomes intimate when you have spent enough late nights in it.

She was also struggling financially — the gap between one job and the next is longer than it looks from the outside, and Pune does not reduce its cost of living out of sympathy. He helped. Rent. Daily expenses. The practical scaffold of staying afloat.

He did not think of it as anything except what you do for someone who matters to you.

She accepted it the way you accept things from someone you trust completely — without ceremony, without keeping score.

Neither of them was keeping score. This would matter later.

Her flat felt like trust made physical.

It was not large. Rented spaces in Pune rarely are. But it had the quality of a place that has been lived in honestly — things where they were put down, not where they were supposed to go. A kitchen that was used. A balcony that served as an extra room. The specific warmth of a space where the performance of living has been dropped.

He was comfortable there in a way he was comfortable very few places.

She was comfortable with him there in a way she was comfortable with very few people.

They talked about everything. Office gossip — the endless supply of it that a workplace provides, the small dramas and alliances and the ones who were definitely going to get fired. Their pasts — her life before this city, his life before this job, the specific pleasure of telling your history to someone who is hearing it for the first time.

She was not a good listener. She had too many of her own thoughts to let someone else's sit undisturbed for long. But she was the right kind of not-good-listener.

He talked anyway. He told her things he did not tell Alstern or Max or anyone in the office gang.

The two worlds were still separate. But the Daayan world was the real one.


  — ✦ —
  She called it fevi quick — the adhesive that bonds permanently.
  She said it as a joke.
  Neither of them laughed for very long.


--- 2.3 — She Got a Job and Disappeared ---

She got a job.

Day shift. A different company, a different building, a different rhythm. The logical next step. The right outcome. I was pleased for her.

Then she disappeared.

Not dramatically. No fight, no final message. The replies just got slower. Then occasional. Then the specific silence of someone who is present in the world but absent from yours.

Three weeks.

I know the exact length because I counted in the way you count things you are trying not to count. Her Instagram showed her at her new office, at a movie with colleagues, at a rooftop somewhere on a Saturday evening, laughing at something I was not part of.

I watched. I did not text.

This cost me something I did not fully account for at the time — the discipline of not reaching, of giving space I had not been asked to give, of performing the kind of friendship that does not need constant confirmation.

I was very good at it on the outside.

On the inside I was running the particular loop that runs when someone you have gotten used to suddenly stops being available. The checking. The interpreting. The specific mental exercise of deciding what silence means.

She called after three weeks.

I picked up.

We talked. At some point I said: you disappeared. I was here, every day, and then you weren't, and I could see on Instagram that you were fine.

She was quiet for a moment. Then she said she was sorry.

I said: I don't need sorry. I just need to understand.

She said: I needed some time for myself. To be somewhere where nobody knew the last few months. To just — be a new person for a while.

I understood this. I did not say so immediately. I let the understanding arrive at its own pace.

She apologised again. I accepted. We continued.

What neither of us said: this was the shape of things now. Close, then distant, then close again. The pattern was already a pattern. It had a rhythm. I was learning to recognise it.

I told myself I was fine with it.

I was not fine with it. But I had not yet figured out what to do with that.


--- 2.4 — Same Building, Different Floor ---

The universe has a sense of humour.

The office shifted buildings. New location, better facilities, management pleased with themselves. Pruthvi was not thinking about the building. He was thinking about the address when he heard it.

Her office was in the same building.

Same building. Different floors. The same lift, the same parking, the same tea tapri on the ground floor.

They started meeting again. The logistics made it inevitable and both of them were, privately, looking for a reason. The same lift, ridden at slightly different times. The tapri on the ground floor where their schedules occasionally overlapped. The parking lot where the day shift ended as the night shift arrived.

Things felt like before. The rhythm reassembled itself without effort.

He was happy about this.

He was not paying attention to what he should have been paying attention to — that the fevi quick phase was over, that she had learned things in its absence, that the person who came back after three weeks was not quite the same person who had left.

He was too busy being glad she was back to ask what she had brought with her.

  The igloo and the early mornings. Her flat and the building stairs.
  Multiple tea stalls where everyone knew their order.
  He helped her stay afloat. She came and went.
  He watched her Instagram for three weeks and did not text.
  She called. He picked up.
  The office moved to her building.
  He called it luck. It was not luck.
  It was the shape of a thing that had decided not to end.


~ ~ ~ DAAYAN ~ ~ ~

  When she got the job she needed space the way you need air after being underwater.

  The fevi quick months had been good — genuinely good, the kind of time that you look back on later and understand was its own complete thing. But they had also been dense. Pruthvi was everywhere. In every hour, every decision, every morning.

  The new job was hers. Her desk, her colleagues, her name on the attendance sheet. Nobody knew anything.

  She posted the Instagram stories intentionally. Not cruelly — she was not trying to hurt him. But she knew he was watching, and she needed him to see that she was fine. That she could be fine.

  She called after three weeks because she missed her friend. Not the version of him that needed her to need him. The other version — the one who had tied her hair on a moving bike at 4 AM, the one who had come back from Rohan's in eight minutes.

  She missed that person.

  She called.

  She did not examine what it meant that she always came back.

  Not yet.

CHAPTER THREE
खींचतान
(The Pull)

======================================================================


--- 3.1 — The Boy on Her Screen ---

She started liking someone.

Pruthvi noticed the way you notice a change in weather — not a single moment but a gradual shift in pressure. She was still there, still present, but her attention had developed a new direction. Someone at her new office. Someone who made her laugh in a specific register he had not heard before.

He told himself it was fine. He was her friend. Friends wanted each other to be happy.

He believed this completely and felt something opposite underneath it.

The unnamed feeling — he searched for the right word for it during the three weeks of watching it arrive. Not ego, though it wore ego's clothes. Not jealousy, though it had jealousy's temperature. Not love, though it had love's specific neediness.

It was closer to: I have given everything this relationship has asked of me. I showed up every time. I came back from Rohan's in eight minutes. I paid rent and held space and picked up every single time she called. And now some person who has been here for five minutes is getting the version of her that I built.

He knew this was not a fair feeling.

It was the feeling anyway.


--- 3.2 — The Replies ---

I am not proud of this part.

I want to say that first, clearly, before I tell it. Not as a disclaimer — as a fact that I have arrived at after enough time to see it properly.

She would come to me when things with him were confusing. This was still the shape of us — when something was difficult, she thought out loud to me. I had been the person she thought out loud to for long enough that it was automatic.

I listened. I was good at listening.

And then I started doing something else.

She would show me his messages. She would ask what to say. And I told her. Carefully. With the specific intelligence of someone who understood exactly which words create distance and was choosing them deliberately while telling himself he was simply helping.

I made her replies. She sent them.

He was talking to her. He was talking to me.

I told myself I was protecting her. From someone who was not right for her. From a situation that would not work out.

I was not protecting her. I was protecting my position. These are not the same thing.

I engineered the ending of that relationship so thoroughly that even if he wanted to return he could not find the door.

I did not plan this. I want to say that too — not as an excuse but as the truth. There was no morning where I woke up and decided to do this. It happened gradually. Each step felt small enough to justify. Each justification led to the next step.

This is how certain things happen. Not with a decision. With a series of small decisions that are only visible as one large decision from the other side of them.

She was free again.

I felt, briefly, something I told myself was relief.

It was not relief.

It was the feeling of someone who has gotten what they wanted and is now standing in the rubble of getting it, realising that wanting something and it being good for you are entirely different categories.


  — ✦ —
  He did not plan it. This is true.
  He also did not stop. This is equally true.
  Between those two facts lives everything that matters.


--- 3.3 — Roles Reversed ---

Then Pruthvi lost his job.

The details are their own story — an accumulation of things, the way job losses usually are, one situation leading to another until the situation becomes untenable.

Four months.

He was jobless for four months. And she — the person whose relationship he had just dismantled from the inside, whose trust he had used as a tool — took care of him. Completely. Every expense, top to bottom. Rent. Food. The daily costs of existing in a city that does not care about your circumstances.

She did this without keeping score.

She did this without mentioning the irony of it — that she was financially supporting the person who had just sabotaged her relationship. Either she did not fully know yet, or she did know and chose this anyway. Either version of her is remarkable.

He let her. He is honest about this now. He let her take care of him while knowing what he had done.

This is the most complicated part of the story. It does not resolve cleanly. It is simply what happened.

Four months have a texture. The first month is the shock of the new situation — the adjustment to time that has no structure, to days that require filling from scratch. The second month is something close to peace, or its imitation. The third month starts to feel like sinking. The fourth is just getting through it.

She was there for all of it.

Not because she owed him anything. Not because she had not noticed things. But because this was who she was — when someone she cared about was struggling, she showed up. Fully. Without calculation.

He understood, during those four months, what it meant to be taken care of by someone who does not need anything in return.

He understood it and did not know what to do with the understanding.

He filed it. The drawer was getting heavy.


  — ✦ —
  She paid his rent and never mentioned it.
  Not once. Not even later, when she had every reason to.
  This is the kind of thing people do
  when they love someone without a name for it.


--- 3.4 — She Learns to Read the Room ---

Daayan was not unobservant.

She had five years on him and the specific intelligence of someone who had learned, early, to read rooms before entering them. She had been filing things of her own. The moments that did not quite add up. The advice that shaped outcomes a little too precisely. The specific quality of his investment in her relationships — too present, too opinionated, too certain about what was wrong with each person she liked.

She did not confront him directly. This was not cowardice. It was the calculation of someone who understood that confronting him would require her to also confront what she had allowed. She had handed him her phone. She had asked what to say. She had not been innocent in the conditions that made things possible.

So she did what she did when something needed to change but she could not change it loudly: she started creating distance. Slowly. Carefully. The way you lower a temperature rather than switching it off.

He noticed immediately.

He did what he always did when he felt her leaving: he held on tighter. More messages. More presence. The specific desperation of someone watching something they do not know how to keep without gripping it.

She pulled harder.

He held tighter still.

Steven watched this for a while. He was the kind of friend who observed carefully and said very little until he had something worth saying. One evening, when the three of them were together and the tension between Pruthvi and Daayan had made the air thick enough to notice, he said something light that broke nothing and defused everything.

Afterwards, privately, he told Pruthvi: yaar, you need to give her room to breathe.

Pruthvi said he knew.

He did not know. Or he knew and could not make his hands do the knowing.


--- 3.5 — The Second Boy ---

I helped her find the next one.

I know how this sounds. I am telling it anyway because the truth of it matters more than how it sounds.

She mentioned someone — a friend of a friend, someone she thought was interesting. She said it the way she said things to me: thinking out loud, without expectation, just sharing a thought.

I encouraged it.

I said: yes, talk to him. He sounds like someone worth knowing.

I believed this while I was saying it. I also, in a part of myself I was not examining, believed that this one would also end. That endings were structural to her relationships. That she would come back as she always had.

I had started treating her departures as temporary by definition.

This is what happens when you stop asking whether someone wants to be there and start assuming they will return. You stop seeing the person and start seeing the pattern.

She started seeing him. I watched.

I fought with her more in that period than in all the years before combined. Not about him directly — about everything adjacent. Her availability. Her priorities. The way she was less present in our conversations. I found reasons to be frustrated that had nothing to do with the reasons I was actually frustrated.

She held the line. She had learned things. She did not let me in the way she had before — did not hand me her phone, did not ask what to say, did not give me the access I had used badly.

I resented this. I am saying it plainly: I resented that she had learned to protect herself. From me, specifically, though I could not say that at the time.

Then his company transferred him. Another city. The relationship ended the way long-distance relationships end when both people are honest about what distance means.

She called me.

I picked up before the second ring.

Steven, when he heard about it later, said only: bhai, you need to look at yourself.

I said I knew.

I was beginning to, actually. The beginning was just the most uncomfortable part.


  ✦  ✦  ✦
  The unnamed feeling with a drawer full of evidence.
  His words in her mouth, sent to someone else.
  Four months where she paid his rent and said nothing.
  Her learning to lock the doors he had previously walked through.
  The second boy, found with his help, defended with everything she had.
  Steven's quiet voice: yaar, give her room.
  She called when it ended. He picked up before the second ring.
  The pattern had a name now.
  Neither of them said it.


~ ~ ~ DAAYAN ~ ~ ~

  She figured out about the replies during the second relationship.

  A conversation where something he said did not match anything she had told him. She held that mismatch up to the light. She looked at it for a long time.

  She did not confront him. She knew that confronting him would require her to also say: I handed you my phone. I let you become the person I asked for advice. I made the conditions that made this possible.

  She had not been innocent. This was the hardest part to sit with.

  So she did not make it about what he had done. She made it about what she was going to do differently.

  She locked the doors.

  When he lost his job she helped him anyway. People ask her — in the version of the story where she tells it — why. She does not have a clean answer.

  The closest she can get is: because it was him. Because underneath all of it, when something was stripped away and there was just a person who needed help, it was him. And she could not not.

  She does not know if this is love or habit or something that does not have a word yet.

  She helped him anyway.

CHAPTER FOUR
टूटना
(The Breaking)

======================================================================


--- 4.1 — The Joke That Was Not a Joke ---

It started with a joke.

They were with the office group — Alstern, Paige, Samuel, Max. The inner circle. An evening that had the quality of evenings that are going well: loose, warm, people at the version of themselves they are when they feel safe.

Pruthvi had built these relationships carefully. With Alstern he had something like respect that had grown into genuine closeness. Paige — didi, he had started calling her, and she had accepted the title in the way of someone who understood what it was being offered. Samuel who knew everything and shared selectively. Max who noticed things and said only what was worth saying.

He had maintained these relationships alongside his life with Daayan by keeping the two entirely separate. The office gang knew she existed. They had met her on occasions — parties where she came because she came with Pruthvi, events where the circles briefly overlapped. But they did not know the shape of things. He had made sure of this.

The two worlds had never touched.

Until tonight.

Alstern made a joke.

About Daayan. About the two of them. The specific kind of joke that has a question inside it dressed as humour — the kind that reveals what someone actually thinks while giving them the exit of having been joking if the reaction is bad.

The room had the quality of a room waiting to see what happens next.

Pruthvi felt something rise in his chest that had nothing to do with the evening or the company or the carefully maintained relationships. It had to do with: she is not here to defend herself. It had to do with: you do not get to do that. It had to do with something older and more accumulated than this room — years of holding things, of filing things, of maintaining the version of himself that managed everything cleanly.

He said something back to Alstern.

Then more.

Then the kind of more that cannot be taken back.

He put it in the group chat too.

This is the detail that made it permanent.


--- 4.2 — His House, Her Alone ---

By the next day it had become a family matter.

They came to his house — the office people, the people who had been in the group chat, the people who had been in the room. They came with the specific energy of people who have decided to resolve a situation and have chosen the resolution in advance.

His father was there.

The two worlds — the one Pruthvi had shown his office gang and the one that existed with Daayan, the two versions of himself he had kept carefully separate for years — were in the same room.

And Daayan was there too.

She had been brought, or come, into a room full of his people. His family, his colleagues, his history. The thing being discussed was her — her and Pruthvi and what their friendship meant and what kind of person Pruthvi was and the version of events that made the most dramatic sense.

She sat in that room.

He was in the room.

Then he was not.


--- 4.3 — ₹3000 and Sandals ---

I want to explain what happened but I am not sure explaining is the right word for it.

I was in the room. My father was there. The people from the office were saying things — presenting a version of events that I recognised as selective, assembling the facts that served the case they had decided to make.

Daayan was in the corner of my peripheral vision.

I could feel the quality of her presence without looking at her. Alone. In a room where everyone else had a side and her side was also, essentially, mine — and I was in the process of being unable to be anyone's side.

I understood I was about to fail her completely. The understanding arrived fully formed, not as a thought but as a physical thing, like something settling in my chest.

I stood up.

I walked out of the room. Out of the flat. Down the stairs. Through the building gate. Into the street.

T-shirt. Hoodie. Jeans. Sandals.

Three thousand rupees in my pocket.

I did not look back. I did not take my phone.

She was still in that room.

I have thought about this more than almost anything else. She was in a room full of my people — my family, my colleagues, my complications — and I had left her there. Not because I did not care. Because I was in the process of becoming someone who could not stay, and staying would have required me to be a version of myself I had already left behind by the time I reached the street.

This does not excuse it.

I called my most trusted friend from a borrowed phone — yes, that is how completely I had left. He met me. He sat with me through the night asking the right questions, which were mostly: now what.

I did not have an answer.

By 4 AM — the same hour the city had first given her to me, on the back of her bike, a different night — I had a direction.

Goa.

I had no specific reason for Goa except that it was far enough to constitute a real departure and I had heard it was the kind of place where a person could disappear without it being unusual.

I left the next morning.

I did not tell her.


--- 4.4 — The Businessman's Five Minutes ---

Goa receives a certain kind of person.

The ones who arrive without luggage. The ones who are walking away from something and have not yet worked out what they are walking toward. The city has a particular patience for this — it has seen enough arrivals of this kind to have stopped being surprised by them.

Pruthvi arrived with three thousand rupees, sandals, and no plan.

He did what people do when they have no plan: he moved. He walked the streets without direction. He sat at places and then left. He found himself returning to the same spots without meaning to — the habit of a person who loops, circling the same points, looking for the thing that makes them make sense.

A man noticed.

Not someone Pruthvi knew. Someone who had simply been in the same area long enough to observe that this person had passed this point several times and was still moving with the quality of someone who is lost rather than someone who is going somewhere.

He asked.

Pruthvi gave him the short version. The argument. The group chat. The house. The walk out. The three thousand rupees. The sandals.

The man listened without interrupting. This was a skill Pruthvi noticed and appreciated.

Then he said: come with me.

He took him to a businessman — someone who had the quality of a person who has assessed many situations quickly and is comfortable making decisions on partial information. He gave Pruthvi five minutes.

Pruthvi gave him five minutes of honesty. Not the managed version, not the version he had been presenting to his office gang for years. The actual version.

The businessman looked at him for a moment after he finished.

Then he said: from today I will take care of everything.

He meant it.

Pruthvi started working. He was good at it — he had always been good at things when he was not consuming himself with something else. He found a routine. He found, in the novelty of a new city and a job that nobody had any context for, a version of himself that had been waiting under the managed version.

He did not contact Daayan.

For the first time in years, he did not reach for the phone.


--- 4.5 — The Pride of Silence ---

Let me tell you what it felt like to not call her.

It felt like standing on a high place and discovering, for the first time, that you are not afraid of heights.

I had spent years in the orbit of her attention. Leaning toward it when it was there, aching when it wasn't. I had cried — more than once, in the private specific way, certain nobody was watching — because she had not replied in two days. I had begged, in the language that is not quite begging but functions identically, for her to stay, to talk to me, to confirm that I still existed in her world.

And now I was in Goa. Working. Sleeping. Eating food I had never eaten before. Meeting people who knew nothing about any of it — not the hair clutcher or the corridor or the photograph or the replies or the job losses or any of it.

Not calling.

Not because I had stopped thinking about her — I had not stopped. Her absence had the quality of a phantom limb: persistent, specific, the ghost of a habit reaching for something that was not there.

But I did not call.

I had decided, in the wordless way of decisions made at 4 AM with sandals and three thousand rupees, that she believed everything was my fault.

And if she believed that, she would not forgive me.

And if she would not forgive me, then the only thing I had left was my silence — which was also, I discovered, something I had not known I possessed.

I was wrong about the belief. I was wrong about the forgiveness.

But the silence itself — the silence was real. And standing in it, after years of reaching, felt like a muscle I had never used discovering that it existed.

I was proud of myself in the uncomplicated way that people are proud of themselves when they have done the thing they thought they could not do.

The pride lasted until she called.

Then I understood that the silence and the peace were not the same thing. I had been confusing them. Standing on a high place is not the same as not being afraid of the fall.


  ✦  ✦  ✦
  A joke that could not be taken back.
  A group chat that made it permanent.
  She sat alone in a room full of his people.
  He walked out with sandals and three thousand rupees.
  A stranger in Goa who noticed he was circling.
  A businessman with five minutes and one sentence.
  The first long silence.
  The pride of it.
  She broke it.


~ ~ ~ DAAYAN ~ ~ ~

  She stayed in that room for forty minutes after he left.

  The door was not locked. Nobody was stopping her.

  She stayed because leaving felt like agreeing with the version of the story being told — the version where she was the problem, where the friendship was a scandal, where two people who had simply been very close to each other were assembled into something else entirely.

  She stayed because she was stubborn and would not give the room the satisfaction.

  When she finally left she went home and sat in her flat in the dark.

  She did not call him that night. She gave him the night.

  When days passed and he did not contact her she understood he had gone somewhere far. She understood it the way she understood things about him — not from information but from the specific shape of an absence.

  She was not going to chase him.

  She had her own life. She had survived things. She would be fine.

  She was fine.

  She called him anyway, weeks later, when something happened that only he would understand.

  This is the part she cannot fully explain, even to herself.

  She has stopped trying.

CHAPTER FIVE
चक्कर
(The Loop)

======================================================================


--- 5.1 — She Called Anyway ---

She called me in Goa.

I had been there for weeks. Long enough to have a routine — a specific path through the day, people who recognised my face at the places I went, a version of normal that belonged to this city rather than the one I had left.

Her name appeared on the screen.

I did not pick up immediately. Not because I was playing a game — I want to be precise about this. I did not pick up because I needed a moment. I had spent weeks building a version of myself that was not oriented toward her. Picking up her call would require me to be, for the duration of that call, something adjacent to the old version.

I gave myself three hours.

Then I called back.

We talked for a long time — the first real conversation in weeks, the kind that fills in the absence with everything that accumulated during it. She did not ask where I was. I did not explain. We just talked, the way we always had, as if the gap were simply a gap and not the thing I had made it.

Somewhere in that conversation something became clear to me. Not as a thought — as a felt thing, the way certain truths arrive.

She will always call.

Not because she is in crisis. Not because she needs something I have and nobody else does. But because there is something between us that she cannot file away either. It lives in her the way it lives in me. Not as a plan or a decision. Just — as a fact.

I stopped being proud of my silence after that call.

Not because the silence had been wrong. But because I understood, finally, the difference between going quiet and being free. I had been treating them as the same thing. They were not the same thing.

Freedom would have meant not needing the three hours.

I needed exactly three hours. Sometimes I needed seven. Sometimes a day.

She knew I would call back. She knew the way she knew things about me — not from information but from the specific quality of our history. She called. She waited. She did not call again.

I called back.

This too was a language. One that required no explanation between speakers.


  — ✦ —
  She called from Goa. He waited three hours.
  He had mistaken going quiet for being free.
  She called and he understood they were not the same thing.
  Freedom would have meant not needing the three hours.


--- 5.2 — The Archive ---

People assume she calls because something is wrong.

Sometimes something is wrong. That was the earlier pattern — the depression, the relationship endings, the specific gravity of a bad period pulling her back to the person who had always been there for the bad periods.

But that is not what it is anymore.

What it is now is harder to name. It is more like: there are things that happened — in corridors and office buildings and Pune streets at 4 AM and Goa and the back of a bike with a hair clutcher in a jacket pocket — that exist as their own archive. A shared record. A thing that belongs equally to both of them and cannot be held by either one alone.

When something touches the archive — a song, a place, a feeling, the specific quality of a late Pune evening that carries a smell she cannot name but recognises — she needs to confirm that it is still there. That it was real. That she did not construct the whole thing from her own imagination.

He is the only one who can confirm it.

So she calls.


--- 5.3 — The Shape of It Now ---

This is what it looks like, years later.

Three months pass. Sometimes four. Sometimes the gap stretches to five or six — the interval is not fixed, which is part of what makes it what it is. A fixed interval would be a schedule. This is not a schedule. This is something that follows its own weather.

Something happens in her life. Not necessarily a crisis — sometimes it is simply a random Tuesday. Sometimes it is a song. Sometimes it is the specific quality of a late evening in Pune that carries a smell she cannot name but recognises.

She picks up her phone.

She calls once. Then once more after a few hours. She does not call continuously — she has her own rhythms, her own ways of waiting. Two calls with an interval between them. She knows he will answer. He knows she knows.

He sees her name.

His heartbeat does what it has always done when her name appears: accelerates. He has not been able to change this. He stopped trying.

He puts the phone face down.

Not because he will not answer. He will answer. He has always answered and he will always answer, and the part of him that is most honest about itself does not want this to be different.

He puts it face down because he needs a moment. To set aside whatever the day has made him. To find, underneath it, the version of himself that she calls for — the one that exists in the archive, the one that knows the whole story, the one she confirmed into existence the first time she handed him her phone in a corridor.

Two hours. Sometimes three.

He gives himself the time.

Then he picks it up.

He does not say: where have you been. He does not say: it has been four months. He does not say: I saw on Instagram that you seem fine.

He says: haan bol.

She says what she called to say.


--- 5.4 — What He Never Changed ---

My phone wallpaper is still a picture of us.

Not the corridor photograph — that one lives in a folder that I know the location of without opening it. A different picture. A moment when we were both laughing at something I cannot fully remember now, both of us mid-expression, neither of us trying to look like anything.

I have had other phones since that picture was taken. I have moved the picture every time.

I have been asked about the wallpaper. Not often — but sometimes someone sees the screen and asks. I say it is a friend. This is accurate. It is also, like many accurate things, not the complete answer.

The corridor photograph: still there. I know exactly where it is in my phone. I have not looked at it recently. I do not need to look at it to know what it shows.

Her, hand on my waist.

Me, arm around her shoulder.

A corridor behind us. A function happening somewhere we were not.

The best picture of ours.


--- 5.5 — The Phone ---

His phone screen lights up.

He is in the middle of something — the middle of something is where he always is when she calls, because life does not pause for the timing to be right. He sees her name.

He picks up the phone.

The wallpaper: both of them, mid-laugh, a moment from a version of Pune that still exists somewhere even if only in here.

He puts the phone face down.

Not because he will not answer. He always answers. The phone glows through the back of its case — her name as warmth rather than text, as presence rather than demand.

Outside, Pune is doing what it does — the perpetual motion of a city that does not wait. Somewhere on the other side of it, in an area where multiple tea stall owners still remember an order that nobody has placed in months, the roads are the same roads.

The building with the pentagon corridors is still there. The corner where two people stood at a strange angle to each other is still that corner. The light in that corridor is ordinary, as it always was.

He reaches for the phone.

He turns it over.

He sees the wallpaper one more time before he answers.

Haan bol.


  ✦  ✦  ✦
  Three months. Sometimes four.
  Her name on a screen. His heartbeat doing what it always does.
  The phone put face down. The glow through the case.
  Two hours where the archive is open and shared and real.
  Then silence until the next time.
  The wallpaper unchanged.
  The corridor photograph exactly where he left it.
  This is not an ending.
  It is what an ending looks like when neither person
  has fully agreed to one.


~ ~ ~ DAAYAN ~ ~ ~

  She does not know he still has the wallpaper.

  She has not asked.

  She knows she no longer has the corridor photograph — lost phones, moved files, the ordinary attrition of digital things over years. She has thought about this and arrived, after some time, at: it is probably better this way. Some things should be kept by the person who needed them most.

  She calls when the archive opens. She does not call every time it opens — there are times she sits with it alone and that is enough. But there are other times when sitting alone with it is not enough, when she needs to hear his voice say something unremarkable and know from the unremarkableness of it that yes, it was real, yes, it still exists, yes, she is not the only one carrying it.

  He always answers.

  She has never asked him why. She does not need to.

  She goes back to her evening after they talk. Her life is full and fine and requires nothing from him to function.

  In a few months — or less, she can never predict it exactly — she will be in the middle of something ordinary and the archive will open.

  She will reach for her phone.

  He will see her name.

  He will put the phone face down.

  He will pick it up.

  This is what they are.

  It is enough.

FOR THE READER
अंत में
(Before You Put This Down)

======================================================================

If you recognised yourself in Pruthvi:

The ledger you kept was real. The care in it was real. The damage was also real. These things can all be true at the same time.

The corridor still exists. The photograph is still somewhere.

What you do with the space between those two facts is the rest of the story.

If you recognised yourself in Daayan:

You were not the villain of someone else's story. You were a person trying to live your life who happened to exist at a frequency someone else could not stop tuning into.

That is not your fault.

It is also not nothing.

The calls you make — the ones you make not because you are broken but because the archive needs to be confirmed — those are honest. There is no shame in honest.

If you recognised yourself in both:

Then you understand that most real stories do not have a side that is simply right. They have two people trying, failing, trying again — and somewhere in the middle of all of that, a photograph.

Still existing.

Still exactly as it was.
