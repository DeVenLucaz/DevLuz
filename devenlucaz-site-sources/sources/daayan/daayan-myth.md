DAAYAN: बंधन
A Novel by DeVenLucaz

======================================================================


--------------------------------------------------
About This Book
--------------------------------------------------
He woke up in a garden that should not exist.

Five doors. Five worlds. Each one shaped like someone he thought he knew. The mist was silver at the edges and warm at the centre, and it smelled like a memory he had not finished making yet. He did not remember arriving. He told himself this was her doing.

He was wrong.

DAAYAN: बंधन is a novel about obsession and mythology and the worlds we build around people who never asked to live in them. It follows Pruthvi through five dreamworld zones — a misty garden, a corridor of mirrors, a flooded basement, an impossible dinner table, a golden gate — each one revealing more of a truth he has spent years refusing to see.

Daayan is five years older and a thousand years wiser, and she has been standing in the middle of his architecture since the beginning, tired, waiting, looking for a door he never built.

Read carefully. The second time you read this book, every sentence will mean something different.


--------------------------------------------------
The People of This Story
--------------------------------------------------

[ PRUTHVI ]

He is in his late twenties. Dark jeans. Dark shirt. Clean-shaven in the way that means he is trying to look like he is not trying. His eyes are tired in a specific way — not from sleeplessness, but from spending too long watching someone who was not watching back.

He is brilliant and he knows it, which is part of the problem. He carries a ring in his pocket — given to him once, kept as proof. He narrates this book in the first person. Every word of his narration is true as he believes it. Very little of it is accurate.


--------------------------------------------------
The People of This Story
--------------------------------------------------

[ DAAYAN ]

She is five years older. Dark saree — always the same dark saree. Gold eyes. A red bindi. Bare feet. Bangles that sound like punctuation — they ring when she moves and are silent when she wants to disappear.

She is not supernatural. The dreamworld has made her supernatural — has taken her particular way of moving through life and blown it into mythology — but the woman herself is simply a woman who lives at a specific angle to the world.

She is not the villain of this story. She has read the villain version of herself and she finds it sad. Like someone built a haunted house out of the fact that she simply existed near them.


--------------------------------------------------
A Note From The Author
--------------------------------------------------
I started writing this story because I needed to understand something and I could not understand it as myself.

There is a specific kind of certainty that feels like love. The certainty that someone is yours in some irreducible way. That you have left a mark on them that time cannot fully clear.

This story is true. The names are not. The feelings are exact.

If you recognise yourself in Pruthvi, that is not a coincidence.

If you recognise yourself in Daayan, I am sorry that someone built a world around you without asking if you wanted to live in it.

Read to the end. The last pages are for the reader who has finished, not the one who has just begun.

— DeVenLucaz

PROLOGUE
बीज
(The Seed)

======================================================================

I did not arrive here.

This is the first thing I can tell you with any certainty: I did not arrive at this garden the way people arrive at places. There was no journey. There was no door I chose to open. One moment there was the ordinary dark behind my eyes, and then there was this — silver mist at knee height, grass so cold it felt like intention, and a sky that had too many stars to be accidental.

I was lying on my back. I remember that.

The mist moved the way water moves when something large has just disturbed it — settling slowly, as though whatever had been displaced was still deciding where to rest.

There were five doors. I could not see them yet — the mist was too thick — but I knew they were there the way you know certain things in dreams: not because you have seen them, but because the knowing arrived with you.

I sat up. My head was lighter than I could remember it being in months. Somewhere in the middle distance a voice was singing — a woman's voice, low and unhurried, the feeling of a song rather than the song itself. It stopped the moment I noticed it.

I should have been afraid. I was not afraid. I was the opposite of afraid.

I should have been more suspicious of that feeling.

I got to my feet. The mist swirled around my knees. A diya was burning somewhere to my left — I could smell it before I could see it, that particular warmth of oil and wick that is older than memory.

The garden began where the mist thinned. Stone walls and old vines and a swing hanging from a tree so dark it seemed made of the night itself. A red cloth on the swing, moving gently in the absence of wind.

And five doors, just as I had known.

I thought: she brought me here. I thought this with complete confidence.

I was wrong about almost everything, but I was most wrong about this.


  — ✦ —
  The seed does not know it is a garden yet.
  It only knows it is in the dark, and the dark is familiar, and it has been here before.

CHAPTER ONE
देहलीज़
(The Threshold)

======================================================================


--- 1.1 — The Garden That Should Not Exist ---

The garden did not follow the rules of gardens.

I recognised this immediately but filed it under the general strangeness of the situation. This was a habit of mine — recognising that something was wrong and then organising the wrongness into a category labelled later, to be retrieved at a more convenient time.

The paths looped. I noticed this after perhaps ten minutes of walking. I had walked in what I was certain was a straight line toward the nearest wall, and arrived back at the diya. The flame had not moved. The oil had not diminished.

I tried again. I chose the path to the right. I walked. I counted my steps. I got to forty-seven before the counting felt absurd, and when I stopped I was back at the diya.

The third time I did not choose a direction. I simply walked, letting my feet decide. I arrived back at the diya in what felt like four steps.

I sat down on the flat stone nearest the diya and looked at the garden properly.

It was beautiful — the first honest thought I had about it. Not beautiful in a comfortable way. Beautiful in the way certain things are beautiful precisely because they are not for you.

It smelled like something I had forgotten on purpose.


  — ✦ —
  A garden that brings you back to its centre no matter where you walk
  is not a garden. It is a decision made by someone else, dressed up in grass.


--- 1.2 — The Swing ---

The swing was significant. I knew this the way you know certain objects are significant in dreams — not because they have done anything yet, but because they are carrying weight they have not yet been asked to put down.

The swing moved. I had been watching it since I sat down by the diya and it had been still — there was no wind — and then, with no preliminary and no creak of rope to announce it, the swing moved.

A slow, unhurried arc. Forward and back. As though someone had just gotten off it.

I crossed to the swing faster than I had meant to. I put my hand on the rope. It was warm — the specific temperature of something that has been held by a person recently.

She likes swings. This was a thing I knew about her — she had mentioned it once, in the way she mentioned things she considered small, the specific peace of sitting on something that was also moving, the way motion and stillness could coexist in that particular way and in almost no other.

I had filed this under things about her. The drawer was full. It had been full for a while.

From somewhere in the garden, the singing began again. Closer this time.


  — ✦ —
  He sat in the seat she had just left and called it closeness.
  The warmth was already gone from the rope. He held it anyway.


--- 1.3 — The Diya ---

I went back to the diya because I had been thinking about the fact that it was still burning.

Oil lamps go out. This is the fundamental truth of oil lamps. The diya I was looking at had been burning since before I arrived and showed no sign of diminishing. The oil in its small clay bowl was exactly as present as when I first saw it. The flame was exactly the same height.

I crouched beside it. In its light, I saw the path — cut into the grass in a line that began at the base of the diya and ran east, toward the stone wall at the garden's edge.

The flame leaned east. The path ran east.

I thought: she lit this for me. I thought this with the certainty of someone who has already decided what they want the evidence to mean and is now working backward from the conclusion.

I stood up. I went east, along the path.

Behind me, the diya continued burning. It would still be burning when I came back. It was burning regardless of whether I came back — for its own reasons, in its own direction.

The flame did not lean toward me. The flame leaned east. I happened to be standing to the east.


  — ✦ —
  He looked at the light and decided it was meant for him.
  All fires lean away from the cold. He was standing in the cold, so he called it warmth.


--- 1.4 — The Riddle on the Wall ---

The stone wall had writing on it. Hindi script, carved deep and filled with gold — not painted, filled, as though molten gold had been poured into letters that had been waiting for it.

The inscription read:

Teen cheezein hain is baag mein jo dikhti hain par hain nahin.

Pehli — woh dastan jo tune khud apne aap ko sunayi.

Doosri — woh wajah jo tune uski wapsi ke peeche dhundhi.

Teesri —

The third line was empty. Not damaged. Not worn away. Empty — the space was there, the indentation was there, but whatever had been carved into it had been removed with such precision that even the depth of the cut remained.

Three things in this garden that can be seen but do not exist.

The first: the story you told yourself.

The second: the reason you looked for behind her returning.

The third: blank.

I read the inscription four times. The fourth time I understood the first two.

The third thing — I stood at the wall for a long time, looking at the blank space where the third line should have been.

I did not know what the third thing was. I know now. But this is the beginning of the book, not the end.


  — ✦ —
  The riddle had three parts. He understood two of them immediately.
  The third was standing right behind him, reading over his shoulder.


--- 1.5 — The White Flower ---

It was lying on the stone path just past the wall. A single white flower, already picked — no stem attached to earth, no roots, no indication of where it had come from.

Three options presented themselves. I could hold it — the gesture of someone claiming something. I could smell it — looking for information the object carried. Or I could set it back down and keep walking.

I stood over it and thought about what each choice said about me.

I made my choice. I picked it up. The flower was cold in my hands — kept out of warmth deliberately. It had nothing to tell me by smell, so I did not try to make it tell me something.

I set it down.

I kept walking.

Behind me, I heard something — not a sound exactly, more the absence of a sound I had expected. Like something settling. Like a breath released.

Later, I would understand what each choice with the flower was worth. Later is the currency this garden runs on.


  — ✦ —
  The flower had no instructions. He gave it some anyway.
  He set it down and called it wisdom. The garden noted what he chose.


--- 1.6 — The Gate ---

She was standing at the gate.

Not standing the way people stand when they are waiting. Standing the way people stand when they have been in a place long enough that the place has organised itself around their presence.

The gate was iron — old iron, the kind that has been through enough weather that the rust and the metal have become indistinguishable. Not locked. The latch was up. The gate was slightly open.

She was facing away from me. Dark purple saree. Her hair in a high ponytail — very still. Everything about her was very still.

Then she spoke. She spoke to the gate, or to the mist beyond it, without turning.

"Aagaye aakhir."

You came, finally.

Not surprised. Not warm. Not cold either — delivered the way you say something you have been saying for years, so many times that the emotion of the original saying has been used up.

She turned. Gold eyes. Red bindi. Bare feet on cold stone. Bangles that rang once with the motion of turning and then were silent.

"Bhool gaye kya," she said. Did you forget.

Not a question. An observation from someone who already knows the answer.

"No," I said. "I didn't forget."

"Mera bharosa karo," she said. Trust me.

"Always," I said, and I meant it completely, and she heard me say it and looked back at the mist — and I had the specific feeling of having answered a question that was not the question she was asking.

She began walking. I followed. Of course I followed.


  — ✦ —
  She said: trust me. He said: always.
  She was asking if he trusted himself. He had not heard that part.


  ✦  ✦  ✦
  A garden where every path returns to the same flame.
  A swing still warm from someone who just left.
  An inscription with one line missing — the third thing, unnameable.
  A woman at a gate that was never locked.
  This is how it begins — not with arrival, but with the certainty that you were expected.


~ ~ ~ DAAYAN ~ ~ ~

  She had been in the garden before he arrived.

  The garden was his, though she had not told him this yet. She could feel the architecture of it — the paths that returned to the diya, the mist that thickened where truth threatened to become visible.

  She was tired. Not dramatically. Quietly tired, in the way of someone who has been standing in someone else's painting long enough to wonder if they are the painting or the painter.

  She had not brought him here.

  She wanted to say this. She wanted to turn when he arrived and say: I did not build this. I did not invite you. This world you are walking through is the inside of your own certainty about me.

  She said instead: aagaye aakhir. Because she had been here a long time, and at least that part was true.

  She said: trust me. She meant: trust yourself, just once, enough to ask the question you keep not asking.

  He said: always.

  These were not the same thing.

CHAPTER TWO
आईना
(The Mirror)

======================================================================


--- 2.1 — The Library of Him ---

The second door opened onto a library.

Not a library someone else had built — a library constructed from my own material. The books on the shelves were not books I had read. They were memories arranged to resemble books. Each spine carried a date rather than a title.

The shelves ran floor to ceiling, and the ceiling was higher than ceilings are permitted to be. The light was deep purple — the purple of very late evening in winter, the colour that light turns when it has almost given up but has not quite.

Between each shelf there was a gilt-framed mirror, and in each mirror the library reflected back at a slight angle — not wrong enough to be alarming, just wrong enough to notice.


  — ✦ —
  A library of his own making, with mirrors at every interval.
  He walked through it looking for her. She was in every reflection. So was he.


--- 2.2 — The Phone ---

The screen showed a conversation. Her messages were there. His were not.

This is the specific horror of looking at a conversation with one side removed: you understand immediately that the side you cannot see was the problem.

Okay. I see. You've said this before. I'm not doing this right now. I said I'm not doing this. I need some space. Please.

These were her messages. I had deleted his. Not out of guilt, I had told myself — out of tidiness.

On the back of the phone, a note in her handwriting:

"Jo bujhta nahi... woh pyaar hai ya adat?"

What does not go out — is it love or is it habit?

The third time I read it, I understood: she was not asking about my feelings for her. She was asking me to examine whether the thing that had not gone out was a living thing or a trained behaviour. Whether I loved her or had simply learned to reach for her so many times that the reaching had become automatic.

I put the phone in my pocket. I did not have an answer.


  — ✦ —
  He found the conversation. One side only, because he had removed the other.
  He already knew what he had said. He just could not read it in her face anymore.


--- 2.3 — The Reflection That Moved ---

The mirror at the end of the corridor had a problem.

It was showing me correctly — but about half a second ahead of where I actually was. Not behind. Ahead. The reflection had already made the movement I was about to make.

She was standing at the entrance to the corridor. Not the way she had been at the gate. Here she was a visitor — standing with the posture of someone who knows they are in a room made of another person's material and is being careful about what they touch.

"Your reflection has been having a much more honest time of it than you have," she said.

Seven versions of this conversation happened in the mirrors around us, each slightly offset.

"In every mirror in this room," she said, "you are a different person. All of them are you." She paused. "You have spent a great deal of time being very certain about which one is the real one."

In the furthest mirror — the smallest one — she was already facing the door.

"Which one do you prefer?" I asked.

She looked at the mirror where she was facing the door. "The one where I can see the exit," she said. Not unkindly. Just accurately.


  — ✦ —
  The reflection knew before he did. The reflection was not kinder — only faster.
  She stood in a room of seven versions of him and looked for the one with a door.


--- 2.4 — The Book With No Title ---

One book on the shelves had no date, no title, no marking of any kind. It sat between two dated volumes like a gap filled with something that refused to be named.

I opened it. The pages were damaged in the sense that something had been removed carefully — not torn, not burned, but excised. I could see the impressions of sentences. Long sentences. The kind people write when trying to explain themselves to someone who is not asking for an explanation.

"That one is from before," she said from behind me.

"Before what?"

"Before you decided what story you were telling about it."

"What kind of story was it?" I asked.

She was quiet for a moment. Then: "The kind where one person is very loud and very present and the other person is trying to figure out whether the loudness means anything or whether it is simply what loudness does."

She turned and walked back toward the corridor of mirrors.

I closed the book. I put it back. It sat on the shelf, still proud of its neighbours, still uninscribed — still carrying the shape of something that had been said very thoroughly and then removed.


  — ✦ —
  The book with no title was the one he had written most carefully.
  He had removed the words. The shape of the sentences stayed. It always stays.


--- 2.5 — The Willpower Mirror ---

At the end of the longest corridor there was a mirror unlike the others. Plain iron framing. Darker glass. It showed — instead of the room reflected — a single fixed scene.

In the mirror: she was standing at a door. Her back to me. Her hand on the door handle. To her right, barely in frame, the shape of a hand — my hand — reaching out. Not grabbing. Just extended. Just there.

She had not turned. She was in the process of leaving, or had always been in the process of leaving.

I stood in front of it for a long time.

This is the thing about certain truths: they are not new information. You have had the information for a long time, arranged around you like furniture — present, functional, navigated around daily.

I looked at the hand in the mirror — the particular quality of the reaching. Hopeful, yes, but also certain. The hand of someone who expected to be taken.

I looked at her in the mirror. At the set of her shoulders. At the hand on the door handle. She was not angry. She was not dramatic. She was simply leaving, with the specific tiredness of someone who has been trying to leave for a long time.

I did not look away. This was the most honest thing I did in the library.


  — ✦ —
  The mirror showed her leaving and his hand extended behind her.
  He looked at it until he understood: the hand was not making her stay. It was reaching into empty space.


--- 2.6 — What The Mirror Actually Showed ---

She found me still standing at the dark mirror.

"You watched it," she said.

"Yes."

"Most people look away."

"I know. I've been looking away for a long time. I'm tired of not seeing things."

"What do you see?" she asked.

I told her everything — the hand and the reaching and the quality of the certainty in it. The tiredness in her shoulders. The door handle. All of it.

She listened. She did not interrupt. When I finished she looked at the mirror once more, then at me.

"Raat ko achi neend aaye?" she said. Did you sleep well at night.

"No," I said. "Not for a long time."

"Hm." She paused. "Main nahi layi tujhe yahan. Tu khud aaya."

I did not bring you here. You came yourself.

It landed in my chest with the feeling of something true that you are not ready to do anything about yet.

"I know," I said. I did not know. But I was beginning to.


  — ✦ —
  She asked if he slept well. It was the kindest thing she knew how to say to someone she had outgrown.
  He said no. For once, the answer fit the question.


  ✦  ✦  ✦
  A library built from his memories, shelved in her order.
  A phone with one side of the conversation missing — the side he removed.
  Seven versions of him in seven mirrors, and she looked for the one with a door.
  A mirror that showed the truth he had arranged his whole life not to see.
  He looked at it anyway. He stayed until it went dark.


~ ~ ~ DAAYAN ~ ~ ~

  She had been in the library longer than he knew.

  The books were his but the organisation was hers, in the way that people we love end up arranging parts of themselves inside us without our full consent. She had not meant to become a library.

  The phone was the thing that made her tired most specifically. She had said please. She did not say please often. She had said it to him in the specific register of someone not asking for a favour but for recognition — please, see what this is doing.

  He had kept sending messages anyway. Not angry ones. That was the difficult part. They were earnest messages. Careful messages. Messages that explained themselves at length, meant everything they said, and would not stop.

  She had been walking through this world for long enough that she had begun to know where the walls were.

  She was very tired of the walls.

CHAPTER TWO AND A HALF
ओढ़नी
(The Blanket)

======================================================================

This is not a zone. There are no doors here, no riddles, no mirrors with independent opinions. This is a memory — the warmest one, the one that explains more about what came before and after than anything in the five zones.

I am setting it down here, between the library and the dinner table, because this is where it belongs.


--- 2.5.1 — That Night ---

Whenever she ran out of things to say, she asked: ab kya. What now.

She could not tolerate the silence that came after a conversation had exhausted itself. Not because she was afraid of silence — but the specific silence of two people who had been talking and had stopped was, to her, a kind of ending she preferred not to let arrive.

So she said: ab kya. What now. And this was my invitation.

I always had something. I said: close your eyes.

This is the story I told her. Not once. Many times. Same opening every time: close your eyes and think it's cold weather.


  — ✦ —
  She asked: what now. He always had an answer.
  The answer was always a door into a smaller, warmer world.


--- 2.5.2 — The Lawn ---

Cold weather. A lawn — short grass that holds the cold the way certain fabrics hold cold, each blade a small conductor of the night's temperature.

We are lying on our backs. The sky above us is fully starred. Moon at the edge of it, crescent. Our breath is visible — each exhale rising and dissipating, each breath its own small cloud with a lifespan of seconds.

We are both shivering. This too I was always specific about: the cold is real in this story. It is not comfortable.

She said, at this point every time: "Thand lag rahi hai." It's cold.

"Main jaanta hoon," I always said. I know.


  — ✦ —
  He always made sure the cold was real in the telling. He thought this was honesty.
  He was honest about the cold. He was not honest about why the story always ended the same way.


--- 2.5.3 — The Blanket ---

And then, in the story: one blanket.

Someone had left it nearby, folded. Large enough for two people if two people were close enough to each other.

The world of the story got smaller here. This was deliberate. I gave it room in the early part — the lawn vast, the sky enormous. And then the blanket arrived and the world closed down to the size of two people on cold grass under a full sky.

Hot inside the blanket. Cold everywhere outside. The stars above, still, because the blanket did not change the stars.

She knew what I was doing. She always knew what I was doing.

She knew every time where the story was going and she listened anyway.

She let me make the world very small and warm. She let me be in it with her. She let me be very specific and slow about it.

And she waited until the moment I thought I had finally reached the part of the story where she would not stop me.

She always stopped me.


  — ✦ —
  He made the world very small. Two people, one blanket, cold everywhere else.
  She let him make it small. She stopped him anyway. This is its own kind of tenderness.


--- 2.5.4 — Daayan Stops Him ---

She always stopped me here. Not with anger — with a smile that contained a great deal of information, the smile of someone who has understood the mechanism of a magic trick so completely that they cannot un-see it and yet still find the performance charming.

"Main jaanti hoon," she said. I know. Every time. I know, Pruthvi.

And then she would open her eyes and look at whatever was above us — and she would not leave.

She stopped the story every time. And every time she stopped it, she stayed.

I told myself this meant the story was working. I was wrong.

She was not staying because the story was working.

She was staying because she liked me — when I was not being this specific person. She did not want to leave right after stopping something; it felt like a punishment, and she was not trying to punish me.

"Main jaanti thi," she would say. I always knew.

"Main phir bhi sunti rahi." I kept listening anyway.

This, too, is its own kind of tenderness.


  — ✦ —
  She said: I always knew. She kept listening anyway.
  He heard this as encouragement. It was her particular form of generosity. He mistook it for permission.


--- 2.5.5 — What He Never Said Out Loud ---

She always knew. She always stopped it. She never left while I was telling it.

I told myself that meant something. I built a case around it.

What I did not ask was: what was she staying for?

Not for the blanket story. She stayed despite the story. Not because of it.

I have been building worlds with words since I was old enough to understand that words could build things. The blanket story was a small world. The dreamworld is a larger one. The architecture is identical.

I kept building. She kept looking for the door I forgot to put in.


  — ✦ —
  He built an entire world on what her staying meant.
  The dreamworld is just a bigger blanket story. She is looking for the exit he forgot to build.


  ✦  ✦  ✦
  Cold grass. One blanket. Her voice saying: I know.
  He made the world small enough to hold her. She stopped him at the threshold every time.
  She stayed anyway — not for the story, but despite it.
  He called it hope. It was her kindness.
  He built a world on the difference.


~ ~ ~ DAAYAN ~ ~ ~

  She remembers the blanket story.

  She has thought about it in the years since and landed, with some difficulty, on: it was the thing he did when he wanted to feel close to her without asking to be close to her.

  What she has thought about more is what it meant that she kept listening.

  She liked the story. Not the place it was going — but the part before that. The cold grass and the stars and the breath made visible. She liked being inside his imagination. It was a compelling place to be.

  She stopped the story every time because the ending he was building was not one she wanted to walk into. But she listened every time because the beginning was genuine.

  She wishes he had known how to stay in the beginning.

  She thinks they would have been fine if he had known how to stay in the beginning.

CHAPTER THREE
नियम
(The Rules)

======================================================================


--- 3.1 — The Table Set For Two ---

The third door opened onto a dinner table.

Not a room with a dinner table in it. A dinner table that was also all the room there was — as far as I could see in any direction, the table continued. White cloth, gold place settings, candles, flowers. Two chairs: one at this end, one at the other — further away than a dinner table had any right to allow.

The chair at the far end was pulled slightly back. Not pushed in. Pulled back at an angle that suggested someone had just stood up, or was about to sit down, or had gotten up to get something from a room that had no elsewhere.

The atmosphere was warm in a way that was wrong. Decaying gold — everything warm and gilded and in the first stages of not being that anymore.


  — ✦ —
  He arrived at a table set for two. One chair empty, pulled back.
  He sat in his chair and looked at hers and told himself she was coming back.


--- 3.2 — The Seven Rules ---

The rulebook was between the place settings. Leather-bound, worn at the corners. NIYAM embossed in gold that had mostly rubbed off.

Inside, seven rules. Below each rule, in smaller writing, the real reason:

Do not look for a door. — Because if I look for a door I might find it.

Do not pick up what is on the table. — Because if I touch it, the illusion will end.

If a flower falls, do not put it back. — Because some things are meant to fall and I always put them back.

If you hear my voice, stop walking. — Because her voice is the only reason I have been moving.

Do not look in any mirror. — Because mirrors have been honest with me and I am not ready.

Do not think about a way out. — Because I do not actually want a way out.

Forget the word exit. — Because the word is a door in itself and I have sealed it.

I read them. Then I looked at the handwriting again — properly.

I had looked at this handwriting in mirrors. I had written in this handwriting on backs of receipts and in margins of books.

The rules were in my handwriting. I had written the rules.


  — ✦ —
  He read the rules and found, beneath each one, the reason.
  The rules were his. The reasons were honest. The handwriting was unmistakable.


--- 3.3 — The Handwriting ---

If the rules were in my handwriting — if I had written them — then the rules were not hers.

I had written: do not look for a door, because if I look for a door I might find it. I had written: forget the word exit, because the word is a door in itself and I have sealed it.

I had sealed the exits.

She was here without having built it. She was inside a world she had not made, navigating rules she had not written, in an architecture entirely of my construction.

She had not brought me here.

I had built a world and she was in it — not because she had chosen it, but because the world had been built around her specific frequency, and things built around a frequency will find what they are tuned to regardless of whether the frequency wants to be found.

From somewhere far down the table, past where the flowers were wilting, she spoke:

"Wapas aana." Come back.

I heard it as an invitation. Later I would understand it was something else — me, saying it to her in my own internal monologue for so long that her voice and mine had become indistinguishable.

I had mistaken my own words for her promise.


  — ✦ —
  He recognised the handwriting. He had hoped he wouldn't.
  Come back — he had said it to her for years in his own head. He had learned to hear it in her voice.


--- 3.4 — The Memory She Tried To Rewrite ---

There was a second notebook on the table. Hers. Her handwriting: economic, precise.

Inside: the same seven events I had recorded in the rulebook. The same dates. But written from the outside — from the perspective of someone watching rather than inhabiting.

The night of the blanket story was there. She had written: he tells me the same story again. I stopped him again. He looked at me afterwards as though the stopping was a setback and not a decision.

I do not know how to say to him that the story is the problem. That the story is always trying to be something and I am always trying to just be here, without the something.

I read this three times. The third time I put the notebook down and looked at the table.

The flowers at the far end had wilted further. Time moved honestly at the far end of the table, while my end was running slightly behind reality.

Her margin note at the bottom, written very small:

"He thinks I am rewriting the memory. I am writing the memory. He is the one who rewrote it."


  — ✦ —
  Her notebook told the same story in a different language.
  He had called it her version. It was simply the version. He had been writing the other one.


--- 3.5 — The Garden Wilting ---

I broke a rule.

Rule One: do not look for a door. I stood up from the table. I walked to the edge of the room. I looked for a door.

There was no door. The rule had been accurate about the possibility, not the certainty. I had written it because I was afraid — not because the door was guaranteed to exist.

I had been afraid of the door.

Through a gap in the zone's architecture: the original garden, visible. Wilting. The swing still. The diya burning lower. The white flowers bent at their stems.

I had done this. By staying, by not looking for the door, by writing rules that sealed the exits — I had done this to the garden too.

She appeared beside me. We both looked at the wilting garden.

"You wrote the rules," I said.

"No," she said.

"The handwriting —"

"Is yours." She said it gently, which was worse than if she had said it sharply. "Tu hamesha khud aata hai." You always come yourself.


  — ✦ —
  He broke rule one and looked for the door. There was no door.
  He had written the rule because he was afraid of the possibility, not the certainty.


--- 3.6 — Three Keyholes ---

Three keyholes in the wall at the room's far edge. Each a different shape — a traditional keyhole, a question mark, and a circle.

Through the traditional keyhole: the flooded basement, dark water, a shaft of light from somewhere above.

Through the question mark: this room, from the other side — seeing it as she saw it.

Through the circle: gold. Just gold. Warm and complete and not yet reachable.

I had no key. Then I put my hand in my pocket and found one.

She stood beside me at the keyholes. She looked through the one shaped like a question mark.

"What do you see?" I asked. "The same table. From a different end."

"Which chair is empty from your end?"

She turned to look at me — the first time in the dreamworld she had looked at me with something other than the patience she had been maintaining.

"Neither," she said. "From my end, both chairs are occupied. The question is which one you're sitting in."

"Wapas aana," she said. This time I heard it differently. Not calling me from far away. Just: come back to the chair you're not sitting in.


  — ✦ —
  Three keyholes. Through the question mark: the room from her side.
  From her end, neither chair was empty. The question was which one he was sitting in.


  ✦  ✦  ✦
  A table set for two, with one chair perpetually empty.
  Rules written in his own handwriting, with his own reasons underneath them.
  A memory she had written from the outside — gently, accurately.
  A garden wilting because he had sealed the exits.
  This chapter is the inside of a decision made so long ago it had started to look like fate.


~ ~ ~ DAAYAN ~ ~ ~

  The dinner table was one of the harder rooms to be in.

  She had seen the rules before — not in writing, but in behaviour. She had watched him not look for the door for years. She had watched him hear her voice and stop moving.

  What the rules did not account for was what happened to the person inside them without having agreed to be. She had not agreed to be the reason someone forgot the word exit.

  She wanted to go home.

  Not dramatically. In the simple, physical way of someone who has been away long enough that home has started to feel like something she might be allowed to return to.

  He had looked for the door. He had found the keyholes. He had the key in his pocket.

  He was beginning to ask the right questions.

CHAPTER FOUR
ज़ख्म
(The Wound)

======================================================================


--- 4.1 — The Flooded Basement ---

The fourth door opened downward.

I descended stairs I could not see the bottom of — not because the stairs were long, but because the dark at the bottom was water. I descended until my feet found it, cold through the leather of my shoes.

The basement. Black water. Grey stone walls. A single shaft of light from above, cutting down into the water and making a column of visibility in the otherwise absolute dark.

Carved into the stone floor, visible through the water: a name. Just a name. Hers.

Her name was on the floor of my basement. Not as an accusation. As a fact. Carved there before the water came — which meant I had carved it.

The water was rising.


  — ✦ —
  Her name carved in the foundation, underwater.
  He built the basement. He carved the name. He was surprised to find it there.


--- 4.2 — The Childhood Room ---

The room was built from the fragments she had mentioned — she had described her childhood in the way people describe it to people they are becoming close to: not as a narrative, but as an atmosphere.

Small bed. Drawings on the walls in crayon. A window letting in grey light. A small bookshelf with books held together by habit rather than binding.

The lock was on the outside of the door.

This was the thing that stopped me: I had come through the door, and when I looked at it from the inside I saw the lock on the outside. A simple lock. Turned.

I went to the door. I opened it from the inside — the lock on the outside was not engaged from in here. But it was there. On the outside. Placed by someone who wanted the option.

"Main kahin nahi jaati," she said from the dark outside. "Main bas hoti hoon."

I don't go anywhere. I just exist.

I stood at the open door and looked at the lock. I had put it there. I had not examined why until now.


  — ✦ —
  He built her childhood room from fragments she had mentioned.
  He put the lock on the outside. He had not examined why until now.


--- 4.3 — The List On The Wall ---

A child's list on the grey wall. Written in the large, unsteady handwriting of a child still learning the relationship between the hand and the letter.

Woh door ho gayi kyunki... She went away because...

Eight reasons. Each one a sentence a child might write when trying to explain why a person has not come back. Each one accurate in the way children's explanations sometimes are — without the sophisticated defences of adult reasoning.

I read seven of them. The eighth was blank — a line drawn for a reason, space left for it, nothing written there.

I understood immediately what was missing.

The eighth reason was: because I would not let her.

Not because I had tried to prevent her from leaving in the physical sense. But in the architectural sense. I had built a world so thoroughly around her presence that leaving it required her to push against something, to assert against the gravity of what I had made.

I had not chained her. I had built a gravity well and called it love.

I picked up the crayon from the floor. I wrote the eighth reason.

The water from the basement was coming under the door.


  — ✦ —
  Eight reasons. Seven written. The eighth space was left blank.
  He knew what belonged there. He had always known. He picked up the crayon.


--- 4.4 — The Drawing ---

On the wall adjacent to the list: a drawing made by someone kneeling or sitting on the floor.

Two figures. One larger, extending a small circle to the smaller one. A ring. Between the two hands: the object in motion, in transit, neither given nor received — permanently in the space between.

The ring was in my pocket. It had been there since before I arrived at the first door.

She had given me the ring once. I had kept it not because I thought she would want it back, but because I thought it meant I could not be fully released. That it constituted a claim.

She appeared in a doorway that had not been there before.

"Tu samajhta hai ab," she said. You understand now.

"I'm beginning to."

"That you gave it to me at a specific moment," I said slowly. "And I decided what that moment meant. And I kept deciding, for a very long time, without asking you if I was right."

She was quiet. The water was at the door now. Rising.


  — ✦ —
  Two figures. One object in transit, between two hands, permanently.
  He had called it a claim. It had been a moment. He had decided what the moment meant.


--- 4.5 — The Void Room ---

Between the fourth door and the fifth there was a room that was not supposed to be there.

No floor. No ceiling. No walls. Just a space cleared of everything so thoroughly that the clearing itself was the room.

She was there. Not dramatically. She appeared the way she appeared in real life — without announcement. And for the first time in the dreamworld, she was not the supernatural version. She was the actual version: a woman who was tired in the specific way of someone who has been carrying something that did not belong to her.

"Tune isme itna gehraai kyun dhundhi?" she asked. Why did you search for such depth in it.

"Because depth seemed like the point," I said. "The point of anything that matters."

"Main toh bas iski qaidi thi," she said. I was just its prisoner.

"Kyunki jaana meri fitrat hai," she said. Because going is my nature. "Tujhse nahi." Not from you.

"Main kahin nahi jaati. Main bas hoti hoon." I don't go anywhere. I just exist.

I sat down in the void. She sat down across from me.

"Pata tha tu dhundhega. Isliye main ruki." I knew you would search. That is why I stayed.


  — ✦ —
  She said: going is my nature — not away from you.
  She said: I knew you would look. That is why I stayed. This was the most generous thing she had ever given him.


--- 4.6 — The Photograph ---

On the floor of the void: a photograph. Face-down.

I reached for it. My hand hovered. This was a different kind of choice from the flower — not about what I was carrying, but about what I was willing to see.

I picked it up. I turned it over.

Two people. A specific moment. Both present in the way people are present in photographs taken before they knew they were being photographed.

The moment I had cited most frequently in the internal argument I had been having with myself for years.

She looked tired in the photograph. Not dramatically. Just: a person who was tired, and had been willing to be in this moment anyway.

I looked at my own face. I was not tired. I was building something. You could see it in the quality of my attention — not the attention of someone present, but of someone cataloguing.

"Theek hai," she said from somewhere. "Khyaal rakhna apna." Take care of yourself.

Not to me. To the version of me in the photograph. To the person who had been too busy building the evidence to be in the moment the evidence was made of.

I held the photograph for a long time. Then I put it carefully in my pocket.


  — ✦ —
  He found the photograph — the one he had cited as proof for years.
  In it, she was tired and present. He was present and building. He finally saw the difference.


  ✦  ✦  ✦
  Her name carved in the foundation he had laid.
  A childhood room built from fragments she mentioned once.
  The eighth reason, finally written.
  In the void: her voice, telling him her nature was not abandonment.
  A photograph of a moment he had been using as evidence.
  He saw it finally.


~ ~ ~ DAAYAN ~ ~ ~

  The void room was the closest thing to hers in the entire dreamworld.

  Not because she had built it. Because the void was what she returned to between one identity and the next — genuinely empty, genuinely quiet.

  She had told him her nature. Going is my nature, not from you — this was as clearly as she knew how to say: your interpretation of what I do is not the same as what I do.

  But he had sat down. In the void. Just sat. Without arguing, without immediately reframing, without building another case from the new evidence.

  He had just sat in the void and been in it.

  This was new.

  She stayed longer than she needed to. Sitting across from someone in a void and not needing to be anything was its own kind of rest.

  She had not rested in this dreamworld since the beginning.

CHAPTER FIVE
सच
(The Truth)

======================================================================


--- 5.1 — The Garden Transformed ---

The fifth door opened onto the first garden.

I recognised it immediately — the stone walls, the old tree, the path I had walked in loops on the first night. But the garden was different. The mist was gold instead of silver. The flowers fully open. The diya burning with a flame larger than before and perfectly still — no lean, no suggestion of wind, just burning in all directions equally.

The air was different. Warmer. But also different in the way that air is different when it has been cleared — when the thing pressing on it has been removed.

From somewhere — from everywhere, from the quality of the gold light itself — her voice. All of them. All the Hindi lines, spoken quietly, simultaneously, like a composition summarising its own themes.


  — ✦ —
  The garden was the same garden. Gold instead of silver. Open instead of closed.
  He had not changed the garden. He had changed what he was bringing to it.


--- 5.2 — The Memory Assembly ---

They came together the way things come together before understanding — all at once, without hierarchy.

The phone. The library. The dark mirror and the reaching hand. The rulebook. Her notebook. The drawing in transit. The name on the foundation. The eighth reason, written. The photograph.

And between them: every time I had heard her voice and called it confirmation. Every time I had continued the blanket story's architecture in a new form. Every time I had been building with her rather than being with her.

I had never just been with her.

She appeared at the gate — the same gate, slightly open, never locked.

"Tu yahan aaya kyunki tu yahan rehna chahta tha," she said. You came here because you wanted to be here.

"Yeh duniya tune banayi." You built this world.

"Main toh bas iski qaidi thi." I was just its prisoner.

The truth. Delivered in the voice of someone who has been waiting to say it — not impatiently, in the way you finally say the true thing when the person in front of you has arrived at the place where it can be heard.


  — ✦ —
  You built this world. She said it and he finally heard it.
  Not because the words were new. Because he had finally stopped building long enough to listen.


--- 5.3 — The Revelation ---

I sat down in the golden garden.

"Was any of it real?" I asked. The question beneath the rulebook and the evidence drawer and the blanket story and the library. The actual question.

She was quiet. Not the quiet of someone considering their answer — the quiet of someone who already has it.

"Tujhe kya lagta hai?" she said. What do you think.

Not evasion. The question turned back with the precision of someone who knows the answer is mine to arrive at, not theirs to give.

I thought: yes, some of it was real. The parts where I wasn't building — those were real. The cold grass and the stars and the breath made visible. The void room, sitting across from each other in nothing.

"Some of it," I said. "The parts where I wasn't building. Those were real."

"Haan," she said. Just yes.

The golden garden was very still. I reached into my pocket. I found the ring. I held it in my palm and looked at it.

Three choices. She stood at the gate and waited and did not press.


  — ✦ —
  Was any of it real? She said: what do you think.
  This was not evasion. It was the only honest way to give him the answer he needed.


--- 5.4 — The Ring ---

I stood up. I crossed the garden. She did not move from the gate.

When I reached her I stopped. The same distance as always — not intimate and not cold. The specific distance of two people who know each other well and have never fully resolved what that knowing means.

I held out my hand. The ring in my palm.

She looked at it. She looked at me.

"Ab ja," she said softly. Now go.

Not an instruction to leave. Permission. You can go. The exit you built rules to prevent — you can take it.

She reached out. She took the ring from my palm.

Not gently and not roughly. The way you take back something that was yours — given out of a moment and then held too long by someone who had turned the moment into a contract.

The garden exhaled.

The gold light shifted slightly. The mist at the edges relaxed. The diya's flame straightened, and for one moment burned in all directions equally — then settled back into its natural lean. East.

"Theek hai," I said. Fine. It was all I had.

She turned. She walked through the gate — not the walk of someone navigating someone else's construction, but the walk of someone finally walking for themselves.

She did not look back.


  — ✦ —
  She took the ring. The garden exhaled.
  The gate was never locked. He just had to stop building around it.


--- 5.5 — The Last Line ---

I stood at the gate for a long time after she was gone.

The dreamworld did not dissolve dramatically. It simply became less insistent. The gold began to soften at the edges. The diya continued to burn because diyas burn until they do not, regardless of the emotional significance of the moment.

I thought about the word she had not said. The word I had been waiting for through all five zones. She had not said it. And that was right.

The last thing she said — from very far away, from the direction the garden opened onto when it finally opened onto something real:

"Ab ja."

Now go.

I went.


  — ✦ —
  The dreamworld did not end dramatically. It simply became less insistent.
  He had been inside a thought. He had finished thinking it. He went.


  ✦  ✦  ✦
  The same garden, golden now, pressure gone.
  All the memories assembling into one true shape.
  She said: you built this. He finally heard it.
  A ring given back. A gate walked through.
  No ceremony in the ending.
  Just: now go. Just: he went.

EPILOGUE
तुम्हारा बंधन
(Your Bond)

======================================================================

What I carry out of the dreamworld:

The phone, with the messages restored. I read them before I left — his messages, present and visible, as they actually were. Not angry. Earnest. Repetitive. I read them and understood why she had said please.

The photograph. I keep it not as evidence but as a record — not of what she felt, but of what I was doing in that moment, visible in my own face if you know how to look.

The memory of the void room. Two people in a space cleared of everything. This is the memory I return to most often. Not because it was the most beautiful moment — but because it was the most honest.

What I do not carry:

The certainty. The specific certainty that she had been pulled back by what I left in her, that my mark on her was the reason for the returning.

This certainty was very comfortable. I had arranged my interior life around its load-bearing qualities.

Without it, there is a structural adjustment required.

I am in the process of that adjustment.

She is not here. She walked through the gate and did not look back.

I do not know where she went. She went wherever she goes, according to her own nature, in patterns I do not have access to and never did.

I hope she got there quickly.

I hope she is resting.

A note on the bond:

Bandhan means the bond between two people — both holding and held. The tie that is also the trap. The thing you place on someone else that, in the placing, also goes around your own wrists.

I had been measuring my bandhan without measuring what it was doing to me.

Jo bandhan tune dusron par daala — woh pehle tujhe hi baandh leta hai.

The bond you place on others — it ties you first.

This is what I know now.

It will have to be enough.

Now that you have finished, I can tell you what the book was about.

The dreamworld was never hers.

डायन did not build the garden or the library or the flooded basement or the dinner table with its rules. She did not summon Pruthvi. She did not trap him.

He arrived because he built the world and then walked into it — and found himself inside his own construction, unable to identify whose architecture it was. Because it is always difficult to recognise the inside of something you built from the outside.

The five zones are not Daayan's psychology. They are Pruthvi's perception of her — filtered through his certainty, his evidence drawer, his particular way of deciding what her actions meant.

She was trapped in his aura. His certainty. His belief in the permanence of his mark on her life.

She was not the villain.

She was a person who existed near someone who loved too loudly — who built too thoroughly — who mistook the architecture of his feeling for evidence of hers.

She was simply a person, in someone else's mythology, trying to go home.

If you read this book and recognised yourself in Pruthvi: the recognition is not an accusation. It is an invitation.

The eighth reason on the wall is always available to be written.

The void room is always available to sit in.

The gate has never been locked.

If you read this book and recognised yourself in Daayan:

I hope the door was there this time.

I hope you found it quickly.
